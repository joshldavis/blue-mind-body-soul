Who you eat with may matter as much as what you eat

Hey friend,

Twelve thousand years ago, a community in the western Galilee buried a woman they believed was a shaman. Around her grave, archaeologists found the butchered bones of three aurochs — enormous wild cattle — and the shells of more than eighty tortoises. Far more food than that small group could eat in a night, all spent at once. It’s the oldest evidence of human feasting we have ever found, and the people who held it weren’t even farmers yet. They had almost nothing, and they spent all of it to mark one death.

What made me want to write this week’s piece is that the modern data has quietly arrived at the same conclusion. The World Happiness Report’s 2025 chapter on shared meals drew on Gallup data from roughly 150,000 people across 142 countries and found that how often you share meals predicts life satisfaction about as strongly as income and unemployment do — across ages, cultures and regions. One additional shared meal per week was associated with about 0.2 points on the ten-point life satisfaction ladder.

This week’s takeaway: The effect isn’t small and it isn’t soft. A meta-analysis of 148 studies covering 308,849 people found that stronger social relationships were associated with a 50% greater likelihood of survival — an effect its authors called comparable to quitting smoking, and larger than obesity or inactivity. Meanwhile, roughly one in four US adults now report eating every single meal alone, up 53% in two decades. We solved the problem of having enough food. Somewhere in there, we let go of the older thing that eating was always also for.

The full piece covers the archaeology of that first feast, why a feast is never really about hunger, what Oxford’s Robin Dunbar found about the specific signature of a bonding meal, the honest caveats about observational evidence — and how to actually lay a feast this week without it becoming a production. It’s adapted from Chapter 8 of The Meals That Built Civilization.

P.S. Honest footnote from the post: nearly all of this evidence is observational, so association rather than proof, and reverse causation is a live concern — happier, better-resourced people plausibly find it easier to gather. And if your circumstances make regular shared meals hard right now, the useful reading of this research isn’t guilt. It’s that one more shared meal a week looks like it’s worth something real, and it counts whether it’s a dinner party or a sandwich with a neighbor.
