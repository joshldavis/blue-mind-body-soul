How to recover from a bad night’s sleep.

Hey friend,

We’ve all had the night — the one where the baby was up, or your brain wouldn’t switch off, or you lay there doing the math on how few hours were left (which only made it worse). You wake up foggy, and your whole body lobbies for two things: sleep in, and get more coffee.

This week’s essay is about why those two instincts are exactly what turn one bad night into a bad week — and what to do instead. The counterintuitive truth from circadian science: you can’t make up lost sleep by chasing it. You recover by protecting the next night. Sleeping in past about an hour drains the sleep pressure you need tonight and shoves your body clock later, so you can’t fall asleep again. And that afternoon coffee? Caffeine’s half-life is five to eight hours — the 3pm cup is still half-active at 9.

This week’s takeaway: The highest-leverage recovery move is free and takes ten minutes. Get up at your normal time, then get sunlight in your eyes within the first hour. That morning cortisol pulse wakes you up and starts the timer that releases tonight’s melatonin. You’re not fixing last night — you’re rescuing the next one.

The full guide walks through the whole protocol: why to delay caffeine 90–120 minutes, how morning movement gives you real (non-chemical) energy, the afternoon coffee trap, and how to recharge with a 20-minute-max nap or a short NSDR session without waking up groggier than you started.

Tool I’m using this week: On a low-sleep afternoon, instead of a second coffee I’ll lie down for a 20-minute NSDR track from Insight Timer. It tops up mental energy with zero risk of the groggy “sleep inertia” a long nap can cause — and unlike caffeine, it doesn’t borrow against that night’s sleep.

One small experiment for the week: the next time you sleep badly, resist the urge to sleep in. Get up on time, step outside for ten minutes, and notice how much easier it is to fall asleep that night. Hit reply and tell me how it goes — I read every message.

Protect the next night,
— Josh

P.S. This is educational, not medical advice. If poor sleep is becoming the rule rather than the exception, talk it through with your doctor — it’s worth ruling out things like apnea.
