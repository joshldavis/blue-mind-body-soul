Sitting is doing more to your brain than your workout undoes.

Hey friend,

This week’s essay is built on one of the most uncomfortable findings in recent longevity research, and I want to give it to you straight: a 2023 JAMA study tracked 49,841 older adults using wrist accelerometers (not self-reports) for a full week, then followed them for nearly seven years. Sitting more than 10 hours a day was linked to sharply higher dementia risk — even after adjusting for moderate-to-vigorous exercise.

The numbers, fully adjusted: 10 hours/day → 8% higher risk. 12 hours/day → 63% higher. 15 hours/day → more than three times higher. The curve is nonlinear — risk barely budges below ~10 hours, then climbs fast above it.

The part that should land hardest: a vigorous morning workout did not rescue the relationship. You can lift, run, eat clean — total daily sitting still predicts dementia risk in a dose-dependent way. The brain doesn’t play by “I exercised, I earned this couch” math, at least not according to this data.

This week’s takeaway: The cheapest, highest-leverage move in long-term brain health is also the most boring — stand up more. Get under 10 hours of sitting per day. Walking pad under a sit-stand desk. Walking meetings. Stand for phone calls. The 30-minute rule: every half hour, stand up and move for 1–2 minutes. Free. Structural. Compounds for decades.

The full essay unpacks the study design, the four leading mechanisms (cerebral blood flow, BDNF, inflammation, cognitive disengagement), a practical protocol you can implement this week, and the important caveats — including what the data can and can’t prove.
