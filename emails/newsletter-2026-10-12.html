Safer bodies, struggling minds

Hey friend,

A statistic stopped me this week. For most of the twentieth century, teenage boys were the group most likely to end up in a hospital with a broken bone. In the early 2010s those rates fell off a cliff — a teenage boy today is slightly less likely to break a bone than his grandfather was.

By any normal measure that’s a win. It also arrived alongside a second curve nobody would call one: across those same years, adolescent anxiety and depression climbed steeply and never came back down.

Jonathan Haidt’s framing is the most useful sentence I can hand a parent right now: we overprotected our children in the real world and underprotected them online. Almost the entire public conversation is about the second half — screens, apps, algorithms. I spent this week on the first half, because it’s the one you can change this weekend.

The key takeaway: What disappeared wasn’t just risk — it was free play, and free play is narrower than we think: chosen by the child, directed by the child, stoppable by the child. Travel soccer isn’t that. A playdate with two hovering parents barely qualifies. And the fear a child meets climbing slightly too high isn’t a bug to engineer out — the research suggests it’s the active ingredient.

In the full piece I lay out a six-rung Independence Ladder — ending with the rung most of us skip, and the one about your own nervous system rather than your child’s readiness.

On that last rung: the honest version of this is that you’ll be anxious watching, and the goal is to train that discomfort rather than obey it. Ten minutes before the errand you’re dreading does more than another round of rules — Insight Timer has thousands of free guided sessions, including several built specifically for parents.
