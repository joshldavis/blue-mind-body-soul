Stories let kids rehearse being good.

Hey friend,

Ask a parent what they most want for their child and almost no one says “a higher reading level.” We say kind. Brave. Honest. Resilient. Character — the quiet architecture of a good life. And it turns out one of the most powerful tools for building it is sitting on a library shelf, costs nothing to borrow, and asks for about fifteen minutes of your evening: a story, read aloud, in your lap.

This week’s essay digs into why it works. When a child hears a story, the brain doesn’t file it as information — it runs a simulation, walking the character’s path and trying on their choices. Research by psychologist Raymond Mar finds that the more fiction kids read, the stronger their theory of mind — the ability to understand that others think and feel differently than they do. In other words, empathy is partly a trainable skill, and story is the gym.

This week’s takeaway: The magic isn’t only the book — it’s the conversation around it. Don’t just read the words; pause once and wonder out loud: “Why do you think she did that? What would you have done?” Then be quiet and let them think. That single habit turns a passive listen into real moral reasoning — and it’s free.

The full essay includes a starter book list matched to virtues by age (honesty, courage, forgiveness, standing up), a simple five-step read-aloud you can start tonight, and the quiet case for why a story does almost the opposite of an endless feed for a developing mind.
