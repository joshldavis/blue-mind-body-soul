You didn’t forget their name. You never stored it.

Hey friend,

You walk into the kitchen and stand there, blank. Someone’s name vanishes the second they say it. You lose ten minutes hunting for the car. As we get older, these little moments can start to feel less like annoyances and more like quiet alarms.

This week’s essay is the reframe I wish everyone had: forgetting is not a malfunction. As Durham University memory researcher Alexander Easton puts it, it’s “an entirely normal part of memory — enabling us to move on or make space for new information.” A brain that held onto everything couldn’t find what matters. Forgetting is the editing. In fact, most everyday lapses aren’t even memory failures — they’re attention failures. You blank on a name because you were distracted when it was offered, so it never got encoded. You didn’t lose it. You never had it.

This week’s takeaway: The fix for everyday forgetting isn’t a sharper memory — it’s better structure. Keys in one place. A calendar you actually trust. The photo of where you parked. Offloading routine details isn’t a failing memory; it’s how you free up attention for what genuinely matters.

The essay also covers the part that matters most: how to tell ordinary forgetting apart from the kind worth a doctor’s visit. Forgetting a name is normal. Asking the same question over and over, getting lost in familiar places, or forgetting how to use everyday objects — those are different, and worth taking seriously. It’s not about how much you forget. It’s about what kind.

Tool I’m using this week: Stress is a quiet memory thief — chronically high cortisol impairs the hippocampus, the exact region that forms new memories. A few minutes of daily practice genuinely helps. I’ve been starting the morning with a short session on Headspace to bring the stress response down before the day floods in. Calmer mind, better encoding.

One small experiment for the week: next time you blank on a word or a name, instead of bracing, just notice it and move on. Odds are you were simply somewhere else when it came in. Hit reply and tell me the most ordinary thing you forget — I read every message.
