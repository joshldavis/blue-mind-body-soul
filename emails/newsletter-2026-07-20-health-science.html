When the experts disagree

Hey friend,

In January 2019, three studies on the same question — does social media harm teen mental health? — published in reputable journals within weeks of each other. Two said no. One said absolutely. If you were a parent that month, you got whiplash. And if you’ve ever felt the same about eggs, saturated fat, red wine, or fasting, you know the feeling well.

Here’s what I’ve come to believe after digging into it: experts disagreeing usually isn’t a sign that science is broken. In this particular fight, both “sides” have largely converged on the same numbers. What they disagree about is whether numbers that size matter — which is an interpretation argument, not a data argument. Almost every health controversy you’ve been confused by lives right there.

This week’s takeaway: Be very careful with the instinct that a “small” correlation is trivial. Smoking and lung cancer, expressed as a raw correlation, is about r = 0.06. Childhood lead exposure and adult IQ is about 0.11 — and we tore lead out of the national water supply over it. Small effects that scale across millions of people and accumulate over years are exactly how public health works.

The full post walks through why blurry measurement makes real effects look weaker than they are, and the six filters I run on any health claim before I let it change my behavior — study type, dose, who it applies to, mechanism, reviews versus single studies, and who benefits from the framing.
