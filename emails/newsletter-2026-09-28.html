The government accidentally proved why bad advice feels personal

Hey friend,

Rate this, 0 to 5, for how accurately it describes you: “You have a great deal of unused capacity which you have not turned to your advantage. At times you are extroverted and sociable, while at other times you are introverted and reserved.”

In 1948, Bertram Forer handed 39 students what he called an individualized personality profile and asked exactly that. The average rating was 4.26. Every student had received the identical paragraph, which Forer had copied out of a newsstand astrology book. It’s been called the Barnum effect ever since, and eight decades of replication have found it gets stronger under two conditions: when you believe the description was made for you specifically, and when it flatters you. Which is, more or less, the design brief for personalized wellness marketing.

But the thing that made me want to write this week’s piece is a document I did not expect to find useful. In September 1995, the American Institutes for Research delivered an outside evaluation of twenty years of government-funded remote viewing — psychic spying. The panel deliberately included one statistician sympathetic to the paranormal and one famously skeptical psychologist, and they reviewed about eighty publications.

This week’s takeaway: The panel found the lab effect was statistically real — and recommended killing the program anyway, because the output was too vague to act on. The reports contained lines like “there is water nearby.” And then this sentence, which I haven’t stopped thinking about: “The nature of the information was sufficiently broad and vague that the analysts typically had to force an interpretation... It was only with this extended interpretation that hits were identified.” The hits weren’t found. They were manufactured, in good faith, by smart people who wanted the information to work. Vagueness wasn’t the flaw — vagueness was the engine.

The full piece walks through why your brain does this (availability, confirmation, and the fact that self-relevant information gets a spotlight), five diagnostic signatures I borrowed straight from that report, where this shows up in modern wellness — metabolic “types,” readiness scores, adrenal fatigue — and a four-question Specificity Test that takes about thirty seconds. Plus the honest caveat: general is not the same as false. “Sleep enough, move most days, eat real food” is extremely general and extremely true. The difference is falsifiability, not breadth.

What’s helping me this week: This piece changed what I’m actually paying for in a supplement. I’m not buying a story about my type — I’m buying verification. [AFFILIATE:THORNE] earns its price because there’s third-party testing and a published certificate of analysis behind the label. That’s a checkable claim, in a category that mostly sells unfalsifiable ones. Which is the whole point of the article, applied to my own cabinet.

One small experiment for the week. The next time a piece of health content makes you feel seen — that jolt of how did they know — stop and reverse the statement. Would the opposite have landed too? If “you push yourself hard and neglect rest” and “you rest well but struggle to push” would both have felt true, it told you nothing about you. You supplied the fit.

P.S. Honest footnote from the post: Forer’s original study was a classroom demonstration with 39 undergraduates, not a rigorous modern experiment — its durability comes from decades of replication, not that one design. And on remote viewing, I reported the panel’s actual position, which is more nuanced than either side likes: a statistically significant anomaly they could not attribute to a paranormal cause, could not explain, and could not use. Reasonable people read that record more favorably than I do.
