You were never too weak to leave

Hey friend,

Ask someone why they still use an app that makes them feel worse, and you usually get an apology. I know. No self-control. It’s an addiction. This week I wrote about a study that suggests we’ve been diagnosing that entirely wrong.

Four economists paid more than a thousand college students real money to answer two questions. First, the standard one: what would it take for you to deactivate TikTok or Instagram for four weeks? Students wanted $59 and $47 — which looks like proof these apps make life better. Then the question nobody had asked: what if everyone deactivated, including you? The sign flipped. Users offered to pay — $28 and $10 — to make the platforms disappear for everybody.

This week’s takeaway: Both answers are sincere. The value of being on a platform depends entirely on who else is there — so your welfare can be negative while leaving alone would be more negative. You stay. Not from weakness. From arithmetic. When researchers asked users why, the top reason wasn’t enjoyment or addiction. It was fear of missing out: 79% for Instagram. That isn’t a craving to be disciplined away. It’s an accurate read of a real social cost — which is exactly why app timers keep failing you.

The full piece covers the maps-app control group that rules out simple anti-tech bias, what this pattern also shows up in (luxury goods, the annual iPhone), the honest limits of the research, and five strategies built for a coordination problem rather than a willpower problem.
