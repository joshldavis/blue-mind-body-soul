The fastest way to calm down is a breath you already take

Hey friend,

“Just take a deep breath” is advice given with good intentions and only half right — and it’s the wrong half. Your heart rate actually rises on every inhale and falls on every exhale. So a big breath in, held in a tense chest, tips you further toward arousal. The exhale is the medicine.

This week I wrote about the pattern that gets it right: the physiological sigh. A full inhale through the nose, a second short sip of air on top, then one long, slow exhale through the mouth. Two or three of those, about fifteen seconds, and you’ll feel the floor come back under you. Your brainstem already runs this program a few times an hour — labs at UCLA and Stanford mapped the circuit in 2016, a cluster of roughly 200 neurons whose entire job is generating sighs to reinflate the air sacs in your lungs. Breathing is unusual in being both automatic and voluntary, which means you can trigger a maneuver your body already trusts, exactly when you need it.

This week’s takeaway: A 2023 Stanford randomized trial put 108 people through five minutes a day of cyclic sighing, box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation, or mindfulness meditation for a month. Everything helped — but cyclic sighing came out ahead for mood, and the breathing groups lowered their resting breathing rate more than the meditators did. One honest footnote the headlines skip: on anxiety specifically, meditation edged ahead. Five minutes a day either way. Less time than making coffee.

The full piece covers why the exhale is the brake, the four moments where I actually use this (before a hard conversation, mid-argument before I say the thing, the 3am wake-up, and before I eat), when to reach for box breathing instead — and an important safety warning about the stimulating breath patterns that people share online without it.
