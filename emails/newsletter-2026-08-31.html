The technology that built your brain is sitting in your kitchen

Hey friend,

A million years ago, deep inside a cave in South Africa, someone built a fire and cooked dinner. We know because archaeologists found the leftovers — burned bone heated to exactly campfire temperature, thirty meters deeper into the rock than any wildfire could reach. This week I wrote about the case that this humble act, repeated over ten thousand generations, is what built the human brain.

The logic is elegant. Brains are metabolically ruinous — yours burns about 20% of your energy, around the clock, on roughly twelve watts. Raw food couldn’t pay that bill; great apes chew up to half their waking hours just breaking even. Cooking is digestion that happens outside the body: heat unlocks calories raw food won’t surrender, and it let our ancestors trade the giant gut for the giant brain.

This week’s takeaway: Cooking didn’t just remodel our bodies — it remodeled our minds. A raw fruit is instant; a root in the embers is a promise. Cooking may be where humans first practiced delayed gratification, nightly, for a million years. And fire invented the evening: the first hours in history spent simply sitting together, talking, around food. We did not evolve to eat alone, standing at a counter, scrolling. The table is the fire now.

The full piece covers the archaeological detective story, the honest scientific debates, the psychology of the hearth — and humanity’s first recipe, three ways: the original fire-pit method, an easy weeknight oven version, and a chef’s homage. It’s adapted from Chapter 1 of the book I’ve been quietly writing, The Meals That Built Civilization — so consider this a first look.
