Dyslexic thinking is not a deficit. It's a different draft of intelligence.

Hey friend,

This week I want to challenge something we almost never question: the idea that a "smart brain" and a "fast reader" are the same thing.

1 in 5 people is dyslexic. That is not a margin of error — that is roughly 20 percent of every classroom, every team, and every dinner party you have ever been to. And the cognitive science is now extremely clear on what most of those people spent their childhoods being told incorrectly: dyslexia is not a deficit. It is a different cognitive style.

The same neural wiring that makes decoding letters harder also makes pattern recognition, imagination, big-picture reasoning, and empathy easier. 84% of dyslexics score above average at imagining. 84% above average at reasoning. 80% above average at connecting with other people. These are not consolation prizes. They are the actual profile — and the dictionary, LinkedIn, and the World Economic Forum have all formally recognized "dyslexic thinking" as a skill in the last few years.

This week’s takeaway: The cognitive style schools historically punished is the cognitive style AI cannot replace. Machines decode text faster than any human now. What they cannot do is imagine the thing that doesn’t yet exist, feel the room, or find the pattern no one was looking for. Those are dyslexic thinking skills — and they belong to all of us when we stop sorting brains into “right” and “broken.”

The full essay walks through the 6 dyslexic thinking skills with the research percentages, the neuroscience of why the wiring trades reading speed for relational and creative bandwidth, the mental health cost of being told you were behind for years, and a practical 6-step playbook for thriving — whether you are dyslexic yourself, raising a dyslexic child, or managing a team that statistically includes more dyslexic thinkers than you realize.
