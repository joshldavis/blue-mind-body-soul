Why Forgetting Is Normal (And When to Worry)

You walk into the kitchen and stand there, blank, with no idea why you came in. You're introduced to someone at a party and their name evaporates before they finish saying it. You spend ten minutes hunting for the car in a parking garage. As we get older, these moments can feel less like minor annoyances and more like quiet alarms — the first cracks in something we'd rather not lose.

Here's the reframe that changes everything: forgetting is not a malfunction. It is one of the things a healthy brain is supposed to do. As Durham University memory researcher Alexander Easton puts it, forgetting is "an entirely normal part of memory — enabling us to move on or make space for new information." Our memories were never built to be a perfect, permanent recording. They were built to be useful. And usefulness requires letting go.

So let's take a clear-eyed look at the evidence: why we forget, why most of it is healthy, and how to tell the difference between the ordinary forgetting of a busy human life and the kind that's worth a conversation with your doctor.

Forgetting Is a Feature, Not a Bug

Imagine if you remembered everything — every license plate you ever glanced at, every conversation word-for-word, every meal, every commute, all of it competing for attention at once. It would be paralyzing. A brain that holds onto everything can't find what matters. Forgetting is the editing process that keeps the signal from drowning in noise.

This is why the rare individuals who genuinely can't forget often describe it as a burden, not a gift. And it's why some of the most distressing mental health conditions are, at their core, disorders of too much remembering rather than too little. We'll come back to that. For now, hold the central idea: a certain amount of forgetting isn't the brain failing. It's the brain working as designed.

The Three Stages Where Memory Can Slip

To understand forgetting, it helps to understand what remembering actually requires. Your brain has to do three distinct jobs, and a slip at any stage shows up as "I forgot."

1. Encoding — getting it in

When information first arrives, your brain can't process all of it. You use attention as a filter, keeping what seems important and discarding the rest. If you're distracted when someone tells you their name, it never gets encoded in the first place. You didn't forget it — you never stored it. This is the single most common "memory failure," and it's completely normal.

2. Storage — keeping it safe

Once encoded, a memory has to be consolidated and held over time. Without reinforcement, the trace fades. This is the "use it or lose it" part of memory, and it has been measured for well over a century.

3. Retrieval — getting it back out

Even a well-stored memory is no good if you can't access it on demand. The classic "tip of the tongue" feeling — you know you know it — is a retrieval failure, not a storage failure. The information is there; the path to it is momentarily blocked.

Attention Is the Real Gatekeeper

Most everyday forgetting traces back to that first stage. We simply weren't paying attention when the information came in. The dinner-party name vanishes because, at the exact moment it was offered, you were focused on shaking a hand, scanning the room, or rehearsing your own introduction. The encoding never happened.

The fix isn't a better memory — it's better structure. Easton points to a beautifully simple example: always putting your keys in the same place. By building a habit, you remove the need to encode the location at all. You'll never "forget" where the keys are because you never have to remember. The most reliable memory systems are the ones you build outside your own head — a consistent spot for your keys, a calendar you actually trust, a notebook, a single inbox. Offloading is not cheating. It's how attention gets freed up for the things that matter.

Use It or Lose It: The Forgetting Curve

In the 1880s, German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus ran one of the first true memory experiments — on himself. He memorized lists of nonsense syllables and tracked how quickly he forgot them. The result, now known as the forgetting curve, is one of the most replicated findings in psychology: without rehearsal, most of what we learn fades within a day or two.

But Ebbinghaus found the antidote in the same experiment. When the syllables were reviewed at spaced intervals, retention shot up dramatically. This is the entire basis of spaced repetition, the most efficient learning technique we know of. Memory isn't a leaky bucket you can only watch drain — it's a path through a field that stays clear the more often you walk it.

There's a catch worth knowing. Every time you recall a memory, you subtly rewrite it. We often remember our last retelling rather than the original event. This is why a story you've told a hundred times can feel vivid and certain even as it drifts further from what actually happened. Confidence in a memory is not proof of its accuracy.

Detail fades, but the gist survives — and that's often enough. Walk out of a store and blank on where you parked, and you can usually still recall whether you turned left or right, whether you were near the edge or the middle. You've lost the precise location but kept the gist, which narrows the search from the whole lot to a small corner of it. Your memory didn't fail. It compressed.

Why Memory Changes as We Age

It's true that forgetting becomes more pronounced as we get older. But more forgetting does not automatically mean something is wrong — and understanding why is genuinely reassuring.

The longer you live, the more experiences you accumulate, and the more those experiences overlap. If you've taken one beach holiday in Spain, you'll recall it with crystal clarity. If you've taken fifteen, separating the trip where your brother came along from the one where it rained all week becomes genuinely hard. This is called interference, and it's a side effect of a rich life, not a failing brain.

Easton offers a perfect analogy: think of your memory like files on a computer. Early on, your filing system is clean and every document has an obvious home. As thousands more documents arrive, folders blur together, things get filed in two plausible places at once, and retrieval slows down — not because anything is lost, but because there's so much more to search through. An older brain holding decades of overlapping memories is doing a harder job than a younger one, and a bit of slowdown is the expected cost.

It's also worth saying plainly: forgetting a name or a date doesn't mean your judgment is going. Older adults often carry deep knowledge and well-honed intuition that more than compensate for the occasional retrieval lapse. A momentary blank is not the same as a decline in thinking.

When NOT Forgetting Is the Problem

The clearest proof that forgetting is healthy comes from what happens when it breaks down in the other direction. In post-traumatic stress disorder, a memory refuses to fade — it stays vivid, intrusive, and disruptive, replaying itself uninvited and hijacking daily life. The problem isn't a weak memory; it's a memory that won't let go.

Something similar shows up in grief and in depression, where negative memories can become sticky and hard to release. In these cases, the ability to forget — to let an experience soften and recede over time — would be a mercy. It's a powerful reminder that the goal was never a perfect memory. The goal is a balanced one: holding what serves us, releasing what doesn't.

When Forgetting Is Worth a Conversation

All of this normalizing comes with an important counterweight. Sometimes forgetting genuinely is an early sign of something that deserves attention, and knowing the difference matters. The distinction isn't really about how much you forget — it's about what kind of forgetting it is.

Red flags worth raising with a doctor

  • Asking the same questions over and over. Forgetting a name because you were distracted is an encoding hiccup. Repeatedly asking a question you've already had answered suggests the information isn't being held at all — a different kind of problem.
  • Getting lost in very familiar places. Misremembering directions somewhere new is ordinary. Struggling to navigate a neighborhood or building you know well points to a deeper difficulty using everyday cues.
  • Forgetting how to do over-learned tasks. Blanking on a dinner guest's name is normal. Forgetting how to use a knife and fork is not. When the how of familiar actions starts to slip, it's worth a professional look.
  • Changes others notice before you do. When friends or family begin pointing out lapses that you yourself aren't aware of, that's a meaningful signal worth taking seriously.

This article is educational, not medical advice. If you're worried about your own memory or someone else's, that worry is reason enough to talk to a physician — early evaluation is always better than waiting and wondering.

How to Support a Healthy Memory

You can't — and shouldn't — stop forgetting altogether. But you can give your brain the conditions it needs to encode well, consolidate properly, and retrieve reliably. The good news is that the levers that protect memory are the same ones that protect the rest of you.

Protect your sleep

Memory consolidation — the move from fragile short-term trace to durable long-term storage — happens largely during sleep. Deep sleep also drives the glymphatic system, the brain's overnight waste-clearance process. Chronically short or fragmented sleep undercuts memory at the exact stage where it's most vulnerable. Seven to nine hours isn't indulgence; it's maintenance.

Move your body

Physical activity increases cerebral blood flow and boosts BDNF, a protein that supports the growth and survival of neurons. Aerobic movement in particular is one of the most consistently brain-protective behaviors we have. Even gentle, daily movement counts — a walk after dinner, a few minutes of mobility work on a Manduka mat between tasks, a habit of standing and stretching every half hour.

Feed the brain real food

The nutrients tied to brain health — omega-3 fatty acids, B vitamins, flavonoids from berries and leafy greens — come first and best from whole food. A brain-forward smoothie (spinach, blueberries, walnuts, a little yogurt) blended in a Vitamix is an easy daily on-ramp. Where real-world gaps exist, a foundational greens product like AG1 or targeted, third-party-tested supplements such as omega-3 and a B-complex from Thorne can help fill them — as a complement to real food, never a replacement.

Lower the stress load

Chronically elevated cortisol impairs the hippocampus, the brain region central to forming new memories. This is part of why you can't think straight when you're overwhelmed — stress physically interferes with encoding. A short daily mindfulness practice with Headspace or Insight Timer is one of the most evidence-backed ways to bring the stress response down and give attention room to work.

Keep learning new things

The "use it or lose it" principle applies to the whole brain. Novel, effortful learning builds cognitive reserve — the brain's resilience buffer against age-related change. The key word is novel: a new language, an instrument, a craft, or a structured course through something like MasterClass challenges the brain in ways that passive scrolling never will.

Build memory outside your head

Finally, stop asking your brain to do jobs a system can do better. Keep keys in one place. Trust a calendar. Write the shopping list down. Take the photo of where you parked. Offloading routine details isn't a sign of a failing memory — it's how you free up attention for the things that genuinely deserve it.

The Bottom Line

Forgetting isn't something to fear, in yourself or in the people you love. Most of it is the sign of a brain doing exactly what it evolved to do — filtering, compressing, and making room. The blank in the kitchen doorway, the vanished name, the lost car: these are the ordinary friction of a full and busy mind, not evidence that it's breaking.

What deserves attention is forgetting that's extreme — repeated questions, getting lost in familiar places, losing the how of everyday actions. That's when the pattern has shifted from normal editing to something worth investigating. Knowing the difference lets you stop bracing against every small lapse and put your energy where it counts: sleeping well, moving daily, eating real food, managing stress, and staying curious. Take care of the brain, and let it forget what it was always meant to forget.
