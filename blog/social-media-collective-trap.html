The Social Media Trap: Why You Can't Quit Alone

Ask someone why they still use Instagram when they say it makes them feel worse, and you'll usually get some version of an apology. I know, I should delete it. I have no self-control. It's an addiction. The whole conversation runs on the assumption that the problem sits inside the person — a defect of willpower to be corrected with discipline, app timers, and shame.

A different explanation comes from an unlikely place: a team of economists who paid real money to find out what social media is actually worth to the people using it. What they found overturns the willpower story. It isn't that you're weak. It's that you're stuck — in a situation where staying is genuinely the better move for you personally, even though almost everyone would be better off if the whole thing vanished. Economists have a name for that structure. They call it a trap.

The Study That Asked a Better Question

Most research on the value of social media asks one question: how much would we have to pay you to quit? People demand real money to deactivate, which gets taken as evidence that the product makes their lives better. If it didn't, why would giving it up require compensation?

In 2023, four economists — Leonardo Bursztyn, Benjamin Handel, Rafael Jiménez-Durán, and Christopher Roth — pointed out the assumption buried in that question. When you ask what someone would need to quit, you're quietly holding everyone else's behavior constant. You're asking: what's it worth to be the one person who logs off while the rest of your social world keeps posting? That's a very different question from what the product is worth compared to a world where it never existed.

So they asked both. Working with more than a thousand college students across several US universities, using mechanisms where the choices carried real financial consequences, they measured two things. First, the standard number: what would you accept to deactivate TikTok or Instagram for four weeks while your friends kept using it? Second, the harder number: how do you feel about a scenario where everyone at your university, including you, deactivates?

The Gap That Changes Everything

On the first question, the results looked like every previous study. Students demanded an average of $59 to give up TikTok for four weeks, and $47 for Instagram. By that measure, 93% and 86% of users were getting positive value from the platforms. Case closed: social media makes people better off.

Then came the second question — and the sign flipped.

When the choice was whether the platform should exist for everyone, users didn't demand payment to see it go. They offered payment. On average, active users were willing to pay $28 to have TikTok deactivated for everyone including themselves, and $10 for Instagram. Sixty-four percent of TikTok users and forty-eight percent of Instagram users were experiencing negative welfare from the product's existence. Non-users would pay even more to make the platforms disappear — $67 and $39 respectively.

Read that twice, because it's genuinely strange. The same person who would need $59 to log off alone would hand over $28 to have the whole thing switched off. Both answers are sincere. Both are rational. They only look contradictory if you forget that the value of being on a platform depends entirely on who else is there.

The researchers' framing is precise: users' welfare is negative, but it would be even more negative if they quit while everyone else stayed. So they stay. Not from weakness. From arithmetic.

Ruling Out the Obvious Objections

My first instinct as a psychologist was skepticism. Maybe people were just performing disapproval of Big Tech — saying what sounds virtuous rather than what they mean. Maybe there's a general distaste for phones that contaminates any question about an app.

The researchers anticipated this and ran the identical experiment on a control product: navigation and maps apps. If the negative result were an artifact of anti-technology sentiment or the way the question was framed, maps should show the same pattern. They didn't. Valuations for maps stayed large and positive throughout, and fewer than 5% of respondents said they'd prefer a world without them. The trap is specific to a certain kind of product, not a general grumpiness about screens.

A separate question asked outright whether respondents would prefer to live in a world without the platform. Fifty-eight percent said yes for both TikTok and Instagram — including 33% of active TikTok users and 57% of active Instagram users. And when students ranked three scenarios — only I quit, everyone quits, nobody quits — "everyone quits" won and "nobody quits" came last. The stated answers lined up tightly with the ones people put money behind.

What's Actually Holding People There

Here's where the study gets psychologically interesting. The researchers asked users who said they'd prefer a world without the platform a blunt follow-up: then why do you still use it? The open-ended answers were hand-coded into categories.

The dominant motive wasn't enjoyment. It wasn't even addiction. It was fear of missing out — cited by 79% of Instagram users and 39% of TikTok users. Entertainment came second (21% and 37%). Self-described addiction mattered more for TikTok (34%) than Instagram (10%). Information was cited by about 15%. Productivity or convenience barely registered at all: 7% and 3%.

One student's answer captures the whole phenomenon: "I feel like if I stop using it, I will be completely out of the loop."

Compare that to the maps control group, where 60% cited productivity and only 10% mentioned anything resembling FOMO. Maps are a tool you use. Instagram, for a large share of people, is a room you can't afford to leave.

This reframe matters clinically, and I don't say that lightly. If the driver is social exclusion rather than compulsion, then a great deal of what we prescribe is aimed at the wrong target. Screen-time limits, grayscale mode, and app blockers all treat the pull of the phone as an appetite problem. But you cannot willpower your way out of a coordination problem. The anxiety of being out of the loop is not a craving — it's an accurate reading of a real social cost.

If FOMO is the engine, the skill worth building isn't restriction — it's tolerating the discomfort of not knowing. It Isn't Only Social Media

To test whether this structure shows up elsewhere, the team surveyed US consumers about luxury goods. Among respondents who owned luxury brands they had bought themselves, 44% said they would prefer to live in a world where those brands didn't exist — and 69% of non-owners agreed.

They also asked about product cycles. Among iPhone owners, 91% would rather Apple released a new model every other year instead of annually — while only 8% wanted a world without iPhones at all. People don't hate the product. They resent the treadmill.

That pattern — I want this to exist, I don't want to be conscripted into competing over it — turns out to describe a surprising amount of modern life.

How You Actually Get Out

If the problem is coordination, the solution is coordination. Individual discipline is the wrong tool, which is why it keeps failing and why the failure keeps getting misread as a character flaw. Here's what the structure of the trap suggests instead.

1. Quit in pairs, not alone

The entire cost of leaving is being the only one gone. That cost collapses the moment someone leaves with you. Don't announce a solo detox — recruit one friend and set the same window. The study's logic is that even a small coordinated group changes each member's math substantially.

Text one person: "I think these apps are making both of us worse. Two weeks, both of us, starting Monday. Deal?" A pact of two is more powerful than a month of solo willpower.

2. Move the relationship, don't end it

FOMO is about losing people, not losing an app. So port the connection before you cut the channel. Export the handful of accounts you genuinely care about into a group chat, a shared calendar, or a standing plan.

Before deactivating, list the five people you'd actually be sad to lose contact with. Get their numbers. That list is almost always shorter than expected — and once it exists, the "out of the loop" fear loses most of its teeth.

3. Separate the tool from the room

Only 3–7% of users cited genuine utility as their reason for staying — but for those uses, the app really is useful. Keep the function, kill the feed. Use direct messages without the scroll. Check the one community that matters on a schedule rather than on impulse.

Log in from a browser instead of the app, on a laptop rather than a phone. The friction is small enough to preserve the utility and large enough to end the ambient grazing.

4. Fill the room with something real

Coordination cuts both ways. If the feed is where your social life lives, removing it leaves a hole — and a hole will pull you back. The most durable exits I've seen replace a virtual room with a physical one: a standing dinner, a weekly class, a training group, a shared project.

Pick something with other humans in it and a fixed time on the calendar. A structured skill you're learning — cooking, writing, an instrument through something like [AFFILIATE:MASTERCLASS] — gives reclaimed hours a shape, and a shape is what keeps them reclaimed.

5. Stop treating the pull as a moral failing

This is the one I'd put first if I could only choose one. The shame layered on top of social media use does real damage and accomplishes nothing. You are responding sensibly to a genuine social cost. Self-criticism doesn't shrink that cost — it just adds a second problem on top of the first.

Swap "why can't I just stop" for "what would make stopping cheaper?" The first question has no answer. The second one has five.

The first week off a feed is usually restless — sleep gets lighter, attention feels jumpy, and everything is harder to sit with. The Honest Caveats

This was a study of American college students — the demographic where social exclusion carries the steepest price. The numbers would likely look different for a forty-five-year-old whose friendships live in text threads and shared calendars. And an economic welfare measure is not a mental health outcome; wanting a product not to exist isn't the same as being harmed by it.

The researchers argue their estimates are conservative rather than inflated — participants who thought the mass deactivation was more likely to actually happen gave more negative valuations, not less. Still, "conservative" isn't "certain." Treat these figures as a signal about the shape of the problem, not a measurement of your own life.

The Part Worth Keeping

The most useful thing this research does is relocate the problem. For fifteen years we've been having a conversation about personal responsibility — your screen time, your discipline, your failure to delete the app. Meanwhile roughly half to two-thirds of users are sitting inside a structure where the individually rational choice and the collectively good outcome point in opposite directions, and no amount of individual virtue resolves that.

Which means the most effective intervention available to you isn't an app blocker. It's a conversation. Ask a friend whether they'd rather both of you were off. The research says the odds are good that they've been waiting for someone to ask.

You were never too weak to leave. You were just trying to leave by yourself.
