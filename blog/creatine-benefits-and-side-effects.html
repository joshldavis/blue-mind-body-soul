Creatine: Benefits, Side Effects, and a Balanced Look at the Evidence

Creatine is one of the most extensively researched and popular supplements in sports and fitness. Known for improving muscle mass, strength, and high-intensity performance, it has become a staple for athletes, lifters, and everyday exercisers. Beyond the gym, researchers are studying creatine for brain function and healthy aging, where the evidence is newer and more mixed. This post covers the benefits, the open questions, and the side effects, so you can make an informed decision.

What Is Creatine?

Creatine is a naturally occurring compound your body makes, mainly in the liver and kidneys, from amino acids, and also gets from red meat and fish. About 95% of it is stored in muscle. It's stored largely as phosphocreatine, which rapidly regenerates adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the primary energy carrier in cells. On a typical diet, muscle stores are only 60–80% full; supplementing raises them by roughly 20–40%, increasing your capacity for short, intense efforts. People who eat little meat start lower and tend to gain more.

Benefits of Creatine Supplementation

1. Muscle Mass and Strength

  • Improved performance: Combined with resistance training, creatine consistently increases strength, power, and muscle mass more than training alone.
  • Mechanism: More phosphocreatine means faster ATP regeneration during intense exercise, so you can do a bit more work per session, and that adds up over weeks.
  • Research evidence: The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) puts typical gains in high-intensity and repeated-effort performance at roughly 10–20% after loading. Responses vary, and people whose stores are already high may notice little.

2. Muscle Recovery

  • Muscle damage: Some studies suggest creatine reduces markers of muscle damage and inflammation after hard exercise, though results are mixed.
  • Training tolerance: Studies in athletes found no increase, and sometimes a decrease, in cramping and injuries among creatine users.

3. Brain Function

  • Memory: The brain uses creatine for energy too. A 2024 meta-analysis of 16 trials found a modest improvement in memory, but not in overall cognition or executive function. Who benefits most is unsettled; vegetarians and people under stress may respond more, and in a 2024 lab study a single large dose partly offset the cognitive slump of a sleepless night.
  • Neurological disease: Early optimism has had to be revised. The largest trial, in more than 1,700 people with early Parkinson's disease, was stopped early because 10 grams a day wasn't slowing the disease. A small 2025 Alzheimer's pilot found higher brain creatine and better scores on some cognitive tests after eight weeks, but it had no placebo group.

4. Benefits for Aging Adults

  • Muscle preservation: Creatine helps counter age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia) when combined with resistance training. A meta-analysis of 22 trials in older adults found about 1.4 kg more lean mass and greater strength than training alone.
  • Bone health: The hope that creatine would protect bone hasn't held up. A two-year trial in 237 postmenopausal women found no improvement in bone mineral density, though some measures of hip bone geometry were better preserved, and a 2026 meta-analysis also found no change in bone density.
  • Cognitive aging: No trial has yet shown that creatine prevents or slows age-related cognitive decline.

5. High-Intensity Exercise Performance

  • Sports performance: Sprinting, weightlifting, team sports with repeated sprints, and interval training benefit most.
  • Versatility: It helps across many kinds of short, intense activity; effects on pure endurance are smaller and less consistent.

6. Safe and Well-Researched

  • Extensive studies: Creatine monohydrate is among the most studied supplements, and the ISSN concludes it is safe for healthy people at recommended doses, in studies lasting up to five years.
  • Regulatory status: The FDA doesn't approve supplements, but it raised no questions about a notice that creatine monohydrate is "generally recognized as safe" as a food ingredient, and European regulators accept that 3 grams a day improves performance in repeated bursts of high-intensity exercise.

Potential Side Effects and Considerations

1. Temporary Weight Gain

  • Water retention: Weight gain is the most consistently reported side effect, typically about 1–2 kg (2–4 pounds) during a loading week as muscles take on water.
  • Not fat gain: The early gain is water; later gains with training are mostly muscle.

2. Digestive Issues

  • Stomach upset: Some people get cramps, nausea, or diarrhea, mostly with large single doses. In one trial, diarrhea was far more common with a single 10-gram dose than with two 5-gram doses.
  • Mitigation: Take it with meals, dissolve it fully, and split larger doses.

3. Kidney and Liver Health

  • Research insights: A 2026 meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials found creatine raised blood creatinine slightly without changing other measures of kidney function. Trials longer than a year are still needed, and liver data are reassuring but thinner.
  • The lab-test catch: Because standard kidney tests use creatinine, creatine can make your kidney function look worse than it is. Tell your doctor you take it.
  • Existing conditions: If you have kidney or liver disease, talk to your doctor before starting.

4. Dehydration and Cramping

  • Mostly a myth: Studies in athletes, including in the heat, found no increase in cramping or dehydration. Staying hydrated is still good practice.

5. Potential Interactions

  • Medications: Few direct interactions are known, but be cautious with drugs that can strain the kidneys, such as regular high-dose NSAIDs, and check with your doctor if you take prescription drugs.
  • Hair loss: This worry traces to one 2009 study that found a rise in the hormone DHT and didn't measure hair. A 2025 randomized trial found no change in DHT or hair.

6. Quality of Supplements

  • Purity matters: Choose plain creatine monohydrate, verified by a third-party tester such as NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport. Be wary of gummies: in 2024 testing by NOW Foods, 5 of 12 gummy products contained little to no creatine.

Best Practices for Creatine Supplementation

  1. Use research-based dosing: Studies typically use a loading phase of about 20 grams a day (in four doses) for 5–7 days, then 3–5 grams a day, or simply 3–5 grams a day, which fills muscle stores in about three to four weeks.
  2. Stay hydrated: Drink water throughout the day.
  3. Monitor your response: Adjust timing or dose if your stomach complains.
  4. Consult professionals: Especially if you have a health condition, take medications, or are pregnant.
  5. Combine with training: Creatine amplifies a structured resistance program; it doesn't replace one.

This article is for educational purposes only and isn't medical advice. Talk to your doctor before starting any new supplement or exercise program, especially if you have kidney or liver disease, diabetes, or high blood pressure, take prescription medication, or are pregnant or breastfeeding. Teenagers should check with a pediatrician first.
