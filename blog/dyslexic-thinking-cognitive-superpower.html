Dyslexic Thinking: The Brain's Hidden Superpower

By Josh May 11, 2026 9 min read Mental Health

One in five people is dyslexic. That is not a margin of error. That is roughly twenty percent of every classroom, every team, and every dinner party you have ever been to. Yet most of us still talk about dyslexia as if it were a deficit — a list of things a child cannot do — instead of what the cognitive science now makes very clear: dyslexic brains do not work worse, they work differently. And in an era where artificial intelligence can write a passable email in three seconds, the way they work differently is becoming one of the most valuable cognitive profiles on the planet.

As a psychologist who has spent two decades inside leadership development, I have watched the conversation shift slowly. The World Economic Forum now lists the skills dyslexic thinkers tend to excel at — lateral thinking, complex problem solving, originality, interpersonal communication — as the top "power skills" for the future of work. The Oxford dictionary added "dyslexic thinking" as an entry. LinkedIn added it as an official skill on profiles. And the research keeps confirming what dyslexic adults have been quietly demonstrating their whole lives: this is not a disability story. This is a cognitive diversity story, and it matters for your wellness, your work, and how you raise the next generation.

Let's unpack what dyslexic thinking actually is, what the brain science says, and how anyone — dyslexic or not — can put these insights to use.

What Dyslexic Thinking Really Means

The classic definition of dyslexia focuses on what is hard: difficulty with reading, spelling, written organization, working memory under stress. That definition is accurate, but it is only half the picture. The newer definition — the one now formally recognized in the dictionary — describes dyslexic thinking as "an approach to problem-solving, assessing information, and learning, often used by people with dyslexia, that involves pattern recognition, spatial reasoning, lateral thinking, and interpersonal skills."

Notice the shift. The first definition lists symptoms. The second describes a cognitive style. The brain that struggles to decode letters quickly is often the same brain that effortlessly sees how a system fits together, anticipates how a customer will feel, or rotates a three-dimensional object in space. Those are not consolation prizes for the spelling — they are core features of the same wiring.

Key insight: Dyslexia is genetic and runs in families, yet every dyslexic person has a different blend of strengths and challenges. The pattern is consistent; the individual profile is not. That is true of most cognitive styles, and it is the most important thing to remember before applying any label.

The Six Dyslexic Thinking Skills

The advocacy group Made By Dyslexia, working with researchers and EY, identified six distinct cognitive strengths that show up more often and more strongly in dyslexic populations. Most dyslexic thinkers have a personal mix — rarely all six — and the percentages below come from EY's research on how often dyslexics score above average on each skill.

1. Visualizing (75% above average)

The ability to interact mentally with space, objects, and ideas — to rotate a system in your mind, see how the parts fit, or watch a project plan come together as if it were a film. Bob Ballard, the oceanographer who discovered the Titanic, has described visualizing the ocean floor in total darkness as his core working method.

2. Imagining (84% above average)

Creating something original, or giving an existing idea a new spin. Imagining is what drives innovation — the leap to a thought no one else in the room has had. Richard Branson talks about daydreaming as a child and then "putting the ideas into reality." Without that imagining muscle, Virgin would not exist.

3. Communicating (71% above average)

Crafting clear, engaging messages — simplifying complexity, telling stories, selling a vision. Dr. Maggie Aderin-Pocock, the space scientist and broadcaster, attributes her ability to make astrophysics feel personal directly to dyslexic communicating: she had to build her own internal translations, and now she can build them for anyone.

4. Reasoning (84% above average)

Understanding patterns, evaluating possibilities, making non-obvious decisions. Dyslexic reasoners often experience the "sudden leap" — connections across data, fields, or disciplines that others do not see until the dyslexic thinker draws the line for them. This is what makes them exceptional strategists.

5. Connecting (80% above average)

Empathy, social awareness, the ability to read a room. Many dyslexic adults developed this early because language-based misreadings forced them to lean harder on tone, body language, and emotional cues. The result is a cognitive style that prioritizes human signals — which is exactly the skill AI cannot replicate.

6. Exploring (84% above average)

Curiosity, comfort with ambiguity, willingness to try the unfamiliar. Dyslexic explorers tend to enjoy unknown territory because they have been navigating unmapped cognitive terrain since school. Discomfort with the new is, for them, less of a deterrent.

Roughly four out of five successful dyslexics attribute their success to these thinking skills rather than in spite of them. That is a remarkable inversion of the "diagnosis" narrative.

The Neuroscience: Different Wiring, Different Strengths

Brain imaging studies consistently show that dyslexic brains process information through different neural pathways than non-dyslexic brains, especially for reading-related tasks. The phonological route — the standard letter-to-sound decoding pipeline — is less efficient. So the brain compensates by leaning on regions associated with imagery, spatial reasoning, and big-picture pattern recognition.

The trade-off is real. Decoding text takes more effort, more energy, and more time. But the same neural rerouting that makes reading harder makes other forms of cognition easier. Dyslexic brains spend less compute on the sequential, rule-based processing that machines now handle for us, and more compute on the relational, contextual, creative processing that machines still cannot.

This is why "dyslexic thinking is a perfect co-pilot for AI" is not just a slogan. AI handles the literal and the repetitive. Dyslexic thinking handles the lateral and the original. Pair them well and the result is genuinely additive.

Where the Stigma Hides — and Why It Matters for Mental Health

One of the most damaging findings in the research is the perception gap. Made By Dyslexia surveys consistently show that around 66% of HR leaders believe their organization supports dyslexic thinkers — and only 14% of actual dyslexics agree. That gap is not a misunderstanding; it is a mental health risk.

Children who are told they cannot read, are slow, or are not trying hard enough internalize that voice. As an I/O psychologist I have seen it again and again in executive coaching: brilliant pattern-thinkers in their forties still carrying a phrase from second grade. The inner critic in a dyslexic adult is often the exact wording of an exhausted teacher or a frustrated parent from thirty years earlier. That is not a moral failing; that is how the developing brain stores feedback.

What this means for self-talk: If you grew up being told you were "behind," even gentle reframes take repetition. Naming the inner voice — "that's the second-grade voice, not me" — is one of the most effective cognitive-behavioral tools we have for this specific pattern.

This is also why "masking" — hiding dyslexic challenges at work — is so common and so costly. Constantly compensating in silence is exhausting and is one of the underrated drivers of burnout in high-performing knowledge workers. Disclosure plus reasonable adjustments consistently outperforms masking for both well-being and productivity.

Famous Dyslexic Thinkers — And Why the List Matters

Richard Branson. Steven Spielberg. Whoopi Goldberg. Barbara Corcoran. Steve Jobs. Octavia Spencer. Erin Brockovich. Tommy Hilfiger. Charles Schwab. Henry Winkler. The list is unusually heavy in entrepreneurs, storytellers, designers, and pattern-thinkers — exactly the categories you would predict from the six skills above.

The point is not that dyslexia guarantees success. It does not. The point is that the cognitive profile that public education historically punishes turns out to be the cognitive profile that founds companies, makes films, and rewires industries. If you grew up thinking dyslexia was a ceiling, look at the data again. It is closer to an architecture.

How to Empower Dyslexic Thinking — Yours or Someone Else's

This is the section I want every parent, every manager, and every dyslexic adult to keep. None of these require a diagnosis. All of them are worth doing.

1. Lead with strengths, name them out loud

Whether you are coaching a dyslexic team member or talking to your own child after a hard school day, name the specific skill you are seeing. "You made a connection no one else made." "You explained that better than the slide did." Specific praise builds identity in a way that generic encouragement cannot.

2. Pair dyslexic thinking with AI, not against it

Tools that handle the parts dyslexic brains find expensive — spelling, formatting, summarizing long documents, reading text aloud — free the dyslexic brain to do what it does best. Treat AI and assistive tech as a wheelchair-equivalent: it does not make the person weaker, it makes the strength accessible.

3. Audit your environments for friction, not for IQ

Most workplaces are still built around fast text-based intake. Long PDF briefs, dense slide decks, written tests. The dyslexic thinker may be the best strategist in the room and still be filtered out by the format. Offering audio versions, voice notes, whiteboard interviews, or extra prep time is a small change with a large yield.

4. Build community

Employee resource groups for neurodivergent employees, dyslexia-aware reading groups for parents, online communities — the isolation is half the cost of an undiagnosed or unsupported dyslexic experience. Connection regulates the nervous system and rewrites the inner critic faster than any individual intervention.

5. Feed the brain that does the thinking

Cognitive flexibility, working memory, and reasoning all depend on the same biology as the rest of the brain: omega-3s, B-vitamins, magnesium, hydration, sleep, and steady glucose. Dyslexic thinkers asked to work hard against their grain are even more sensitive to under-fueling than the average brain.

The Bigger Reframe

For most of the last century, "intelligence" was implicitly defined as how quickly a person could decode and recombine text. Standardized tests rewarded it, schools selected for it, and dyslexic children — and the adults they grew into — paid the price.

The next century will not work that way. Machines decode text faster than any human. What they cannot do is imagine the thing that does not yet exist, feel the room, find the pattern that no one was looking for, or tell a story that changes someone's mind. Those are dyslexic thinking skills, and they belong to all of us when we stop sorting brains into "right" and "broken."

If you or someone you love is dyslexic, please hear this clearly: you are not behind. You are wired for a different draft of intelligence, one the world has finally started naming. The work now is to lean into the strengths, build scaffolding around the challenges, and stop apologizing for either.
