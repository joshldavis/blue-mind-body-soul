Caption

Teenage boys break fewer bones than their grandfathers did. I can't stop thinking about what that cost us. 🧹 For most of the last century, teen boys were the group most likely to end up in an ER with a fracture. Then, in the early 2010s, those rates collapsed. Today's teenage boy is slightly LESS likely to break a bone than his father was. Across the exact same years, adolescent anxiety and depression climbed steeply. Safer bodies, struggling minds. Jonathan Haidt summarizes the shift in one sentence I think every parent should have: we overprotected our children in the real world and underprotected them online. Most of us are arguing about the second half. I want to talk about the first, because it's the part we can actually change at home. What disappeared wasn't just risk — it was free play. And free play is narrower than we think. Real play is chosen by the child, directed by the child, and stoppable by the child. Travel soccer isn't free play. Enrichment class isn't. A playdate with two hovering parents barely qualifies. They're fine things. They just don't do what play does. Free play is where a kid practices emotion regulation with real stakes, negotiates without a referee, and learns the single most protective belief in developmental psychology: my choices move the world. And here's the part that reframes everything — the fear a child meets in play isn't a bug to engineer out. It's the active ingredient. Ellen Sandseter's research suggests risky play is anti-phobic: a kid who climbs a little too high and manages it has just run a self-dosed exposure protocol. Remove those experiences and the fear doesn't disappear. It never gets processed. The way back is a ladder, not a leap. One real errand alone. One protected unscheduled block. One real tool, taught properly. And — the rung nobody does — two other families who'll do it with you, because a kid sent outside alone in an empty neighborhood isn't getting play. Main Post

Here's a statistic that reorganized how I think about childhood. For most of the twentieth century, teenage boys were the single group in America most likely to end up in a hospital with a broken bone. Falls from trees, bike crashes, collisions in a field nobody was supervising. It was simply what adolescence looked like. Then, in the early 2010s, those hospitalization rates fell off a cliff. Today a teenage boy is slightly LESS likely to break a bone than his father or grandfather was. By any conventional measure that's a triumph. And it happened alongside a second trend nobody would call one: across the same years, adolescent anxiety and depression climbed steeply and have not come back down. The social psychologist Jonathan Haidt summarizes the shift in a sentence I think is the most useful framing available to parents right now: we overprotected our children in the real world and underprotected them online. Two errors, made simultaneously, each for what felt like good reasons at the time. Most of the conversation focuses on the second half — screens, apps, algorithms. I want to talk about the first half, because it gets less attention and may be the more fixable of the two. What disappeared from childhood wasn't just risk. It was play. And play is narrower than we usually mean: chosen by the child, directed by the child, stoppable by the child. Travel soccer isn't free play. Enrichment class isn't. A playdate with two hovering parents barely qualifies. These are fine things — they simply aren't the thing, and they don't do what the thing does. Free play is where children practice emotion regulation with real stakes, negotiate without a referee, and build the belief that their own choices move the world. No adult can teach those directly. And the fear a child meets while playing isn't a bug to be engineered out. Researcher Ellen Sandseter's work suggests risky play is anti-phobic — a child who climbs slightly too high, manages it, and comes back down has just run an exposure protocol at a dose they selected themselves. Remove those experiences and the fear doesn't disappear. It simply never gets processed. The way back isn't a revolution. It's a ladder: one real errand alone. One protected unscheduled block a week. One real tool, taught properly. And the rung most of us skip — two other families who'll do it with you, because a child sent outside alone in an empty neighborhood isn't getting play, they're getting solitude. 💬 Question for you: what's one thing you were allowed to do alone as a kid that you haven't let your own children do yet?
