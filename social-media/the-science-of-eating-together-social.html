Slide 1 (Hook): How often you eat with other people predicts your life satisfaction about as strongly as your income does. 🍽️ (Yes, really.)
Slide 2: 12,000 years ago, in a cave in the Galilee, a community buried a woman they believed was a shaman. Around her grave: the bones of 3 wild aurochs and 80+ tortoise shells. Far more food than they could eat in a night.
Slide 3: That's not dinner. That's the debris of a FEAST — the oldest one we've ever found. And the people who threw it weren't even farmers yet. They spent a fortune of food, all at once, to say goodbye to someone who mattered.
Slide 4: A feast is never really about hunger. It displays surplus. It builds bonds beyond the family. It creates obligation (now YOU have to feed ME). And it marks the passages: a death, a wedding, a harvest.
Slide 5: Here's where it gets uncomfortable. The World Happiness Report 2025 analyzed Gallup data from ~150,000 people in 142 countries. Meal-sharing frequency predicted life satisfaction on par with income and unemployment. Across every culture tested.
Slide 6: One extra shared meal per week ≈ a 0.2-point gain on the 0–10 life satisfaction ladder. That's roughly a country moving up five spots in the global happiness rankings. From one dinner.
Slide 7: Oxford's Robin Dunbar found the same thing from the other end: people who eat socially more often are happier, more trusting, more embedded in their communities, with more friends they can lean on. The bonding meals had a signature — more people, more laughter, more reminiscing.
Slide 8: And the mortality data is not subtle. 148 studies, 308,849 people: those with stronger social relationships had a 50% greater likelihood of survival. Comparable to quitting smoking. Bigger than obesity or inactivity.
Slide 9: Meanwhile: in 2023, about 1 in 4 American adults reported eating EVERY meal alone the previous day. Up 53% in twenty years. We solved the problem of "enough" and quietly lost the thing eating was always also for.

Thread (8 tweets)

1/ How often you eat with other people predicts your life satisfaction about as strongly as your income does. That's not a wellness slogan. It's the finding of a 150,000-person, 142-country analysis. Thread on the science of eating together — and the oldest feast ever found. 🍽️
2/ Start 12,000 years ago, in a cave called Hilazon Tachtit in the western Galilee. A community buried a woman — disabled, ~45, buried with a tortoise shell, an eagle wing-tip, a leopard pelvis, a human foot that wasn't hers. Almost certainly a shaman. One of the oldest such burials known.
3/ But the striking part is what surrounded the grave: butchered bones from at least 3 aurochs (enormous wild cattle) and 80+ tortoise shells. Far more meat than that group could eat at a normal meal, concentrated around a single burial event. That's a feast. The oldest one we've found.
4/ A feast is never about hunger. It does four things at once: • displays surplus (you can't feast without more than you need) • builds bonds beyond the family • creates obligation — the guest now owes the host a feast in return • marks the passages: death, marriage, harvest, alliance
5/ Now the modern data. World Happiness Report 2025, Ch. 3: Gallup data, ~150,000 people, 142 countries. Meal-sharing frequency predicted life satisfaction on par with income and unemployment — across ages, genders, cultures, regions. One extra shared meal/week ≈ +0.2 on the 0–10 ladder.
6/ Mechanism, from Robin Dunbar (Oxford, 2017): people who eat socially more often are happier, more trusting, more engaged locally, with more friends they can depend on. The meals that bonded people had a signature: more people, more laughter, more reminiscing. Path analysis suggests eating together → bondedness, not just the reverse.
7/ And the hard endpoint. Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010: 148 studies, 308,849 people, 7.5-year average follow-up. Stronger social relationships = 50% greater likelihood of survival. Comparable to smoking cessation. Larger than obesity or physical inactivity.
8/ Meanwhile we're running the opposite experiment. 2023: ~1 in 4 US adults reported eating every meal alone the previous day. Up 53% in two decades. The intervention is unglamorous: make too much food on purpose, put it in the middle, invite someone. Full piece → [LINK]

Main Post

Twelve thousand years ago, in a cave in the hills of the western Galilee, a community buried a woman they believed was a shaman. She was about forty-five, walked with a limp, and was laid to rest with a tortoise shell, the wing-tip of a golden eagle, and the tail of a wild bull. But the detail that stopped me is what surrounded her grave: the butchered bones of three aurochs and the shells of more than eighty tortoises. Far more food than that small group could eat in a night — all of it spent at once, at the moment they said goodbye to her. It's the oldest evidence of human feasting we've ever found. And these people weren't farmers yet. They had almost nothing, and they spent all of it to mark one death. I've been sitting with that image, because the modern research has quietly arrived in the same place. The World Happiness Report's 2025 chapter on shared meals drew on Gallup data from about 150,000 people in 142 countries. It found that how often you share meals predicts life satisfaction about as strongly as income and unemployment do — across ages, cultures, and regions. One additional shared meal per week was worth roughly 0.2 points on the ten-point life satisfaction ladder. Separately, a meta-analysis of 148 studies covering 308,849 people found that strong social relationships were associated with a 50% greater likelihood of survival, an effect the authors compared to quitting smoking. And meanwhile: in 2023, roughly one in four American adults said they had eaten every single meal alone the previous day. That's up 53% in twenty years. I want to be fair to the solo meal — sometimes it's genuinely restorative, and I'd defend that. But 26% of adults eating every meal alone isn't a self-care trend. That's a structural change in how a society feeds itself.
