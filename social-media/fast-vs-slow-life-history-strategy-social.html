Caption

There's a psychological trait that predicts your health, your relationships, and how long you're likely to live — and it's not what you think. 🧠 Evolutionary psychologists call it "life history strategy," and they've spent 40 years measuring it. Everyone sorts somewhere on a spectrum from FAST to SLOW. Fast strategy: more partners, earlier risk-taking, live for right now, less investment in any single relationship or outcome. Slow strategy: fewer, deeper relationships. Delayed gratification. Heavy investment in a smaller number of long-term outcomes — including your own future health. Here's the part that made me sit up: a 2014 meta-analysis pooling thousands of participants found slow-strategy scores correlate with higher conscientiousness, stronger executive function, more relationship stability, lower substance involvement AND better self-reported physical and mental functioning on a standard medical health survey. Where does your strategy come from? It's roughly 65% heritable. But the rest is environment — and specifically, how predictable and safe your childhood was. Kids raised in chaos develop faster strategies. It's not a character flaw. It was the correct answer to the environment that produced it. The genuinely useful part: your PRESENT environment is something you can still shape, no matter what your childhood looked like. Predictability, practiced delay, and investing deeply in a few close relationships all pull the same lever. Full research + a practical 5-step guide on the blog. Link in bio.

Main Post

Some people plan a decade out and haven't done anything reckless in years. Others move through life at a sprint. We usually describe this as a difference in maturity or discipline. Evolutionary psychologists describe it more precisely — they call it your life history strategy, and they've spent 40 years building a science around measuring it. Every strategy sits somewhere on a spectrum from fast to slow. A fast strategy allocates resources toward immediate reward: more partners, earlier risk-taking, less investment in any single relationship. A slow strategy allocates resources toward long-term stability: fewer, deeper relationships, delayed gratification, and heavy investment in a smaller number of outcomes, including your own future health. Here's what made me want to write about it. A 2014 meta-analysis pooling data across thousands of participants found that a "slow" score correlates with higher conscientiousness, stronger self-control, more relationship stability, lower rates of delinquency and substance involvement — and better self-reported physical and mental functioning on a standard medical health survey. Where does your strategy come from? Roughly 65% is heritable. The rest is environmental, and the research is consistent: children raised in unpredictable, unstable conditions develop faster strategies. It's not a flaw. It's the correct adaptation to an environment where the future can't be counted on. The useful part is that your present environment is still yours to shape, whatever your childhood looked like. Manufacturing predictability, practicing delay in a low-stakes area on purpose, and investing deliberately in a small number of close relationships all pull the same lever — toward the strategy the research consistently links to a longer, steadier life. 💬 Curious where you'd place yourself on that spectrum — and whether it matches how you were raised.
