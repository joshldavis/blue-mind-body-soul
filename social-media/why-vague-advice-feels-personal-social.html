Caption

That feeling of "how did they KNOW" is not evidence. It's a manufacturing process. 🧠 In 1948, Bertram Forer gave 39 students what he called an individualized personality profile and asked them to rate its accuracy 0 to 5. Average: 4.26. Everyone got the same paragraph, lifted from an astrology book at a newsstand. Seventy-eight years of replication later, we know two things make the illusion stronger: believing it was prepared for you specifically, and content that flatters. Which is, more or less, the entire design brief of personalized wellness marketing. But here's the version that actually changed how I read things. In 1995 the American Institutes for Research delivered an outside evaluation of the government's 20-year remote viewing program. The panel deliberately included one statistician friendly to the paranormal and one famously skeptical psychologist. They found a statistically significant effect. Real numbers. And they still recommended killing the program — because the output was too vague to act on. The reports said things like "there is water nearby." So the analysts forced an interpretation, and only then did hits appear. That's the whole mechanism, documented at government scale. Give a motivated reader something broad enough and they'll supply the specificity themselves, then experience the fit as proof. Five tells, taken from that report: ● broad and general ● inconsistent across independent sources ● never says anything that could be checked and found wrong ● padded with irrelevant volume that reads as thoroughness ● the burden of making it fit falls on you To be fair — general isn't the same as false. "Sleep enough, move most days, eat real food" is extremely general and extremely true. The difference isn't breadth, it's falsifiability. A real claim could have failed and didn't. "Your energy is blocked" can never fail, and only tells you to buy something. Full piece — including the four-question Specificity Test — link in bio. Main Post

In September 1995, three researchers delivered a report that ended a program the US government had quietly funded for two decades. It was about psychic spying. And it is the most useful thing I've read all year about why bad wellness advice feels like it was written for you personally. Here's what the panel found. The lab numbers were real. Across 154 experiments and more than 26,000 trials, the departure from chance was enormous. The reviewers said so plainly. And they recommended shutting the whole thing down. Not because the statistics were fake — because of what happened when anyone tried to use the output. The reports were, in the panel's words, "stated in broad, vague terms." Things like "there is water nearby." So the analysts receiving them — intelligent, trained professionals who wanted the information to be useful — did what human beings do. The report puts it exactly: "The nature of the information was sufficiently broad and vague that the analysts typically had to force an interpretation... It was only with this extended interpretation that hits were identified." The hits weren't found. They were manufactured, in good faith, by the readers. Psychology already had a name for this. In 1948, Bertram Forer handed 39 students what he called an individualized personality profile and asked them to rate how accurately it described them, 0 to 5. Average: 4.26. Every student received the same paragraph, which Forer had copied out of a newsstand astrology book. It's been called the Barnum effect ever since, and it gets stronger under two conditions: when you believe the description was made for you specifically, and when it flatters you. I don't think most of us are being harmed by horoscopes. The expensive versions are better dressed — quiz-gated supplement funnels, metabolic "types," readiness scores, diagnostic labels that sound clinical and aren't. Adrenal fatigue is the durable example: recognized by no endocrine society, no supporting evidence in a systematic review of 58 studies, and still everywhere. Because its symptom list is tired, foggy, crave salt, hard to get going. Which describes essentially all of modern adult life. Being fair about it: general is not the same as false. "Sleep enough, move most days, eat real food, stay close to people" is extremely general and extremely true. The difference isn't breadth — it's whether the claim could have been wrong. A tested claim could have failed and didn't. "Your energy is blocked" can never fail, and only ever tells you to buy something. Four questions I now run on anything aimed at me personally: 1. Would the opposite statement also have felt true? 2. What would I have to observe for this to be wrong? 3. Who supplied the specific details — them or me? 4.
