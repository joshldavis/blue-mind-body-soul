Slide 1 (Hook): Three studies. Same question. Same month. Same peer-reviewed journals. Completely opposite conclusions. Here's what's actually going on when experts "disagree." 🧠
Slide 2: January 2019: two studies said social media had little effect on teen mental health. One said the effect was real and serious. Parents got whiplash. Sound familiar? Eggs. Fat. Wine. Fasting. Screens.
Slide 3: The uncomfortable truth: experts disagreeing usually doesn't mean the science is broken. It means the disagreement is about something more specific than the headline lets on.
Slide 4: In the social media debate, both "sides" found nearly the SAME numbers — correlations around r = 0.10 to 0.15. They don't disagree about the data. They disagree about whether a number that size matters.
Slide 5: So does it? Consider: smoking and lung cancer, as a raw correlation, is about r = 0.06. If "small correlation = ignore it," we'd have concluded smoking was fine. 🚫
Slide 6: Two things turn a "small" effect into a big one: SCALE (tiny per person, huge across millions) and ACCUMULATION (an hour a day, every day, for six years).
Slide 7: Also: most of this research measures both ends blurrily — people guessing their screen hours, short mood questionnaires. Blurry measurement mathematically SHRINKS correlations. The small numbers are a floor, not a ceiling.
Slide 8: Filters 1–3 I run on any health claim: 1. What KIND of study? (Correlational can't prove cause. Experiments can.) 2. What DOSE? (Almost nothing is binary.) 3. WHO is it for? (Averages hide subgroups.)
Slide 9: Filters 4–6: 4. Is there a MECHANISM? (No pathway = be suspicious.) 5. Find the REVIEW, not the study. (Search + "meta-analysis.") 6. Who benefits from the FRAMING? (Certainty sells. Nuance doesn't.)
Slide 10 (CTA): When the evidence is unsettled, weigh the cost of being wrong. Cheap + low-risk + good for you anyway? Just do it. Expensive or invasive? Raise the bar. 👉 Full breakdown on the blog — link in bio.

4/ The bigger surprise: both "sides" have largely converged on the same numbers. Correlations of roughly r = .10 to .15. They don't disagree about the data. They disagree about whether a number that size matters. That's an interpretation fight. Almost all of them are.

3 Standalone Tweets

Smoking and lung cancer, expressed as a raw correlation, is about r = .06. If "small correlation means it doesn't matter" were true, we'd have concluded smoking was fine. Be very careful with that heuristic.
Most health research measures both ends blurrily — people guessing their hours, short self-report questionnaires. Blurry measurement forces correlations DOWN. Always. So when a study reports a weak association, ask: weak, or blurry?

Main Post

Have you ever gotten whiplash from health headlines? Eggs are good, eggs are bad. Screens are destroying kids, screens are fine. Red wine adds years, red wine takes them away. It's easy to conclude that nobody knows anything and you should just ignore all of it. I want to offer a different read, because I think the whiplash is telling us something useful. In January 2019, three studies on social media and teen mental health published in reputable journals within weeks of each other — and reached opposite conclusions. Rather than write another op-ed, psychologists Jonathan Haidt, Jean Twenge and Zach Rausch opened a public document and started collecting every study they could find, including the ones that undercut their own view, and invited their critics to comment in the margins. Reading it carefully changed how I read research. Two things stood out. First: the two "sides" had largely converged on the same numbers. What they disagreed about was whether a number that size mattered — an interpretation fight, not a data fight. That's true of most health controversies. Second: our instinct that a "small" correlation is trivial is badly wrong. Smoking and lung cancer, expressed as a raw correlation, is about 0.06. Childhood lead exposure and adult IQ is about 0.11 — and we ripped lead out of the national water supply over it. Small effects that scale across millions of people, and accumulate over years, are exactly how public health works.
