Caption

Everything we say about social media and willpower may be aimed at the wrong target. 💡 In 2023, four economists ran an experiment with real money on the line. First they asked the standard question: what would we have to pay you to deactivate TikTok or Instagram for four weeks? Students wanted $59 and $47. By that measure, ~90% were getting positive value. Then they asked the question nobody asks: how do you feel about EVERYONE deactivating, including you? The sign flipped. Users offered to pay $28 and $10 to have the platforms gone for everyone. 64% of TikTok users and 48% of Instagram users were experiencing negative welfare from the product simply existing. Both answers are sincere. They only look contradictory if you forget that the value of a platform depends entirely on who else is on it. The top reason people stay? Not entertainment. Not addiction. FOMO — 79% of Instagram users. One student put it perfectly: "If I stop using it, I will be completely out of the loop." As a psychologist, here's the part I keep thinking about: we've spent fifteen years telling people this is a discipline problem. But you can't willpower your way out of a coordination problem. The shame we pile on top accomplishes nothing except adding a second problem to the first. The way out isn't an app blocker. It's a conversation. Ask one friend if they'd rather you were both off. The research says the odds are good they've been waiting for someone to ask. 🤝 Full piece — including the 5 strategies that actually match the shape of this problem — on the blog. Main Post

Here's a result I can't stop thinking about. In 2023, four economists ran an experiment with more than a thousand college students, with real money attached to the answers. First they asked the standard question: what would we have to pay you to deactivate TikTok or Instagram for four weeks? Students wanted $59 and $47. By that measure, nearly everyone was getting positive value from these apps. Then the researchers asked a question nobody had thought to ask: how do you feel about a world where EVERYONE at your university deactivates — including you? The answer reversed. Users no longer wanted to be paid. They offered to pay — $28 to have TikTok switched off for everyone, $10 for Instagram. Sixty-four percent of TikTok users and forty-eight percent of Instagram users were experiencing negative welfare from the product simply existing. Both answers are honest. They only look contradictory if you forget that the value of being on a platform depends entirely on who else is there. The researchers put it plainly: users' welfare is negative, but it would be even more negative if they quit while everyone else stayed. So they stay. Not from weakness — from arithmetic. When they asked users directly why they keep scrolling, the top answer wasn't entertainment and it wasn't addiction. It was fear of missing out — 79% for Instagram. One student wrote: "I feel like if I stop using it, I will be completely out of the loop." As a psychologist, this reframes the whole conversation for me. We've spent fifteen years treating this as a discipline problem, prescribing app timers and screen limits and a great deal of quiet shame. But you cannot willpower your way out of a coordination problem. The pull people feel isn't a craving — it's an accurate reading of a genuine social cost. Which means the most effective thing available to you isn't an app blocker. It's a conversation. 💬 Question for you: if you could press a button and make one platform disappear for everyone — you included — would you press it? And which one?
