Caption

Your ancestors were microbiologists—they just didn't know it yet.

For thousands of years, humans kept sourdough starters alive, soured cabbage into sauerkraut, and aged fish sauce with zero idea that microbes even existed. They worked by intuition: do it this way and it's good, do it that way and it spoils.

Now science has caught up—and given us a humbling new reason to care. The same fermented foods our ancestors made for preservation and flavor turn out to nourish the trillions of microbes living in our gut. And that inner ecosystem shapes our digestion, our immunity, even our mood.

The best part? The simplest fermentation is one of the easiest, safest things you can make. Cabbage + salt + time = one of the world's great foods.

Primary Post

I've been going down a fascinating rabbit hole on fermentation, and it's changed how I think about my kitchen—and my gut.

Here's the idea that got me: fermentation is "managed decay." To keep food from going bad, you let it go bad on purpose, in exactly the right way, so the good microbes take over and the dangerous ones can't survive. That's how we get sauerkraut, kimchi, yogurt, kefir, miso, and sourdough.

What amazed me is that humans mastered this for thousands of years without any idea microbes existed. They worked by intuition and tradition—a whole body of practical microbiology, passed down long before Pasteur explained why any of it worked.

And now science has added a humbling reason to care: the microbiome. The trillions of bacteria in your gut shape your digestion, your immunity, and even your mood. The same fermented foods our ancestors made for preservation turn out to nourish that inner ecosystem too.

The best part? The simplest version is one of the easiest, safest things you can make. Cabbage and salt become one of the world's great foods.

I wrote a full guide on the blog—the science, the history, and three ways to start at home.
